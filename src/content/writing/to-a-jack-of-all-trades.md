At one point I was all in on being a finance major, influenced more than I'd admit by an unspoken deal that if I wasn't going to live out my immigrant parents' dream for me to become a doctor, I'd be that Wall Street business baddie and secure their second-safest option.

Somewhere along the way, I developed an immovable gut instinct to follow my heart and study software engineering. But I had backup plans to deal with any doubts that came my way, most of them in the form of some cross-discipline endeavor. It felt right to take advantage of what appeared to be a "gift" of well-roundedness, just in case I ended up not actually being that good at the thing I felt like I loved most. I'd attribute it to loss aversion.[^1]

With most things in life, where nobody's deciding for you, and lightning doesn't strike when you shout at dusk to give you a sign, it's easy to feed your fear of better options (FOBO). It might have us spiraling into a superficial pursuit of keeping doors open, trying to prove that choice can be delayed if we're exceptional enough. Ironically, the whole jack-of-all-trades malaise can stem from feeling unexceptional at everything.

---

A finance-adjacent concept is _optionality_, the right to take an opportunity if things go well, with no obligation if they don't. I came across an essay by Mihir A. Desai in _The Harvard Crimson_, who had a sharp take on his students' attempts to maximize optionality. His point is that people may seem to reasonably set up safety nets for their dream, yet collecting those safety nets forms a habit that changes them to become merely "the sum total of those choices" while their dream drifts into the background.[^2] (The essay is from 2017, but its ideas, unlike options, don't expire. Highly recommended read.)

It almost sounds practical to start shutting doors to keep out the curse of optionality. But I've come to frame it that some things are _doors_ to walk through, while the rest of our interests can be _windows_ still letting the world in, as context and experience that make us stronger in our specializations.

Not everything has to have a goal of equal depth and ambition. I paint to add colour to my life. I sing and play sports only because the fun outweighs my self-awareness. I lift to uplift. I'll puppeteer my coding agent to get things done and I'll let myself understand what it wrote more than enough for the time being and not nearly enough for my own fulfillment. I write to reflect.

I'll let myself read about market microstructure or the biological clocks of wildlife at 5am for no reason at all. I'll remind myself that it needs neither a plan nor a reason, and that I don't need to become an expert on everything I catch in passing. Read more, and satiate curiosity for its own sake, and let curiosity guide you.

---

It's been argued that generalists do better in fields where the rules change frequently, and that sampling options early is how people find what's right for them.[^3] I don't disagree; we sample through windows and peepholes. A problem arises when we sample endlessly, or when we tell ourselves every window is a door. We'll end up too full and lethargic to fit through any door, and still never have the actual meal.

I think it's quite beautiful how life asks us to endure boredom before it reveals depth. When we do try to narrow in, it goes a long way to know the difference between being selective and prioritizing, versus taking shortcuts and skipping the hard parts. The hard part isn't always starting. Just as often, we lose the plot halfway through a project, or get bored of running our daily 3k.[^4] And even when we keep at it, physically showing up isn't enough. What's in our heads is what makes or breaks it.

You can own an idea and not have the mindset of a founder. Train a model, yet know little about machine learning. Have thoughts but never ponder.

---

"Jack of all trades" is often used as a jab at someone who spreads their focus too thin. When it comes to learning new things, for instance, novelty might get us to the top of the hill, from where we dabble in moguls of topics on the way down, speeding through to the bottom where we look up at a flat sheet of white. (I don't even ski, I just like extended metaphors.)

The longer version people like to quote is:

> A jack of all trades is a master of none, but oftentimes better than a master of one.

Still, I have a hunch, as I look at the people I admire, that being a master of one still unlocks a piece of self-actualization that a jack of all trades may never quite get to know. Life is short enough that oftentimes, though not for everyone, experiencing it demands your choice and commitment to just one.

There's also this idea that "how you do anything is how you do everything," and I've never felt more perceived by a saying. But notice there is no ask, and no rush, to blindly go do everything, and I see a corollary that, if you can _do_, in an idiosyncratic definition of _do_, one thing, then may you do anything.

Let us keep chasing that one.
