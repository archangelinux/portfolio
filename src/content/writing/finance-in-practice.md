People say finance is more of an art than a science and I can see why. It's a bit of law and a bit of math intertwined within the beauty of verifiable logic, sometimes overthrown by sound interpretation. It's as traceable as recurrence relations, like when the opening balance calls back to the last closing one, or when tax credits telescope down supply chains, but disorienting in a way recursion never is, because nothing guarantees each step carries forward the way the one before it did, with revisions, restatements and policy changes.

Even after committing to the University of Waterloo for Software Engineering, I was still interested in pursuing something interdisciplinary. I didn't really know what that would be. At one point I thought maybe finance and accounting could be the safety net come the day that AI steals our SWE jobs (as if junior accounting is any more promising). I've also come to understand that not every field I take an interest in must be a door to be forced open. They can be windows that simply let the world in as context and experience that make us stronger in our specializations.

> [!KEY]
> AI as the job thief is a bit of a red herring, if it really is the lack of domain expertise and managerial control over the tools meant to empower us that pulls the rug out from under us.

Beyond personal finance, my main exposure to finance as of date has been high school accounting courses and watching my parent work as a tax manager. I think finance is a lot more fun to study through resolving everyday inquiries rather than from page 1 of a textbook, be it filing taxes, interpreting financial statements, personal investing, and learning about the industry through work experience. My next step to build domain knowledge in finance as a software engineer will be my internship at Wealthsimple for the next four months.

### "Stock markets should go up over time. Here's why"

It's the same mechanisms that governments employ to uphold capitalism.

- **Risk premiums.** The built-in financial reward the market must pay investors over time to compensate them for enduring short-term losses. `Risk Premium = Expected Return − Risk-Free Rate`.
- **Interest rates.** The government-controlled baseline for borrowing that directly dictates whether cash flows into safe bank accounts or risky stocks. When they keep safe interest rates low, they deliberately force investors out of cash and into the stock market to chase better returns.

Smart risks usually pay off in the long run for these reasons. Short-term negative returns are expected, so diversify and remain dispassionate. Never try to time the market, for good or bad days; there really are only patterns in retrospect.

### "How to invest when the market is high"

- Don't panic sell unless your portfolio is very equity heavy and savings are needed within the next 6 to 25 months.
- Corrections (market drops of less than 20%) take an average of 115 days to recover.
- Invest steadily and regularly (every month). Dollar-cost averaging helps remove emotions from the equation completely.
- Studies show that returns for those who invest when markets are at record highs are not significantly different from returns for those who invest at other times.
- Rebalance portfolio allocation from time to time: 90/10 stock to bond split if young, 60/40 if retiring.
- The P/E (price to earnings) ratio for US stocks is actually 40% higher than that of stocks from the rest of the world[^1], so some exposure to international stocks could help.

### "How to invest when the market is low"

> [!INFO] bear market
> A decline of 20% or more for a consistent period. They tend to be connected to recessions, during which interest rates go down while bond prices and unemployment go up. A big drop may not actually be a bear market; bear markets don't have a particular duration either (historically 33 days to over 5 years).

- Honestly just be diversified across different economic environments; don't try to time the bottom nor panic sell.
- **Tax-loss harvesting:** selling stocks that you lost money on for a very similar one to get a tax write-off while basically keeping your position in the market, so you are invested when the rebound occurs.

### "Remember: it's all part of a bigger cycle"

| Phase | What's happening | Returns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Despair | Bear market as investors anticipate recession; sharp decline in valuations. Corporate earnings are slowing but not terrible. | Negative |
| Hope | Recovery anticipation before profits actually bottom out. Captures significant returns before valuation shoots back up. | Strong |
| Growth | Performance starts catching up to valuation; earnings grow as expected when the economy was weak. Long phase of modestly positive returns. | Modest, steady |
| Optimism | After a long expansion, over-optimistic investors drive expectations to levels companies often can't meet. Returns can be very high but this phase often ends in recession. | High, then… |

[^1]: Per Apollo Global Management, as cited in the newsletter.
